Before class: choose one experiment

The 18-minute route

0–2 minutes · Predict

What stays fixed?

Stage 2 · Fixed population. Show the five possible card types and probabilities.

“When we draw another group of three, does the pool change? Must every group average the pool mean?”

Have learners name one random object before drawing and one fixed model quantity. Briefly demonstrate replacement: a card type can appear again, even within one group.

Listen for this distinction
The probability model and μ are fixed. Xᵢ is the random power at draw position i before observation. Repeating does not deplete the pool. A possible sample need not have mean μ; a rare 40 is possible and mathematically legitimate.
2–5 minutes · Experiment

Three observations become one estimate

Stage 3 · One sample. Check Hide worked answers; choose Draw one sample. Ask learners to calculate from the three displayed powers, then reveal.

FIXED PARAMETER · 参数Population mean μ
RULE BEFORE SAMPLING · 估计量X̄=(X₁+X₂+X₃)/3
OBSERVED RESULT · 估计值This sample's x̄

“Is the rule the same object as its numerical answer? Has this sample changed the population mean?”

Expected calculation and language
[8,40,2] gives x̄=50/3≈16.667. X̄ is the statistic/estimator before the draw;16.667 is its observed estimate. μ remains 5.3. An unbiased estimator does not promise that the next observed value equals μ.
5–8 minutes · Observe

One whole group becomes one dot

Choose Next stage → to reveal stage 4 · Sampling distribution. Use Find sample #1's point →. Draw again; use Revisit sample to recall sample 1.

“Does this dot represent one card, or all three cards after applying the rule? Which objects changed on the second draw?”

Explain the two encodings: bars show relative frequency in statistic bins; dots in the separate lower strip identify complete saved samples. Horizontal dot position is the statistic. Vertical dot separation is layout, not probability .

A useful counterexample
Second x̄≈5.333. After two draws, the average of the two observed estimates is 11. The theoretical E(X̄)=5.3 is not forced to equal the finite simulation average. At R=1, one occupied histogram bin has 100% empirical frequency; its saved dot is still just one sample.
8–11 minutes · Repeat

Build the empirical sampling distribution

Select Repeated samples R +200, then Run batch. Pause if needed; inspect a saved group.

“What does n count? What does R count? Does adding repetitions make one future group of three less variable?”

Keep the parameter, population, n and estimating rule fixed. Compare Theoretical E(T) with Simulation mean; their meanings differ even if the numbers happen to be close.

Do not promise finite equality
Starting from R=2, adding 200 yields R=202. n counts observations per sample; R counts independent repetitions of the complete sampling experiment. Increasing R improves Monte Carlo depiction; the theoretical SE of the next estimator is unchanged. Expectation averages over all possible samples with their probabilities.
11–14 minutes · Explain

Exact possibilities have unequal weights

Stay at stage 4. Change to n=2; explain that the old cohort clears. Enable Exact small model, then expand Inspect exact ordered samples & weights.

“There are 25 ordered pairs. Are they equally likely? Compare the two-lowest and two-rarest pairs.”

Inspect [2,2] on Page 1. Set the inspector’s Page field to 3 for [40,40], case 25. Compare the total beneath the table, the original values, product probability and weighted contribution. If the full table overloads the group, compare just two rows and the exact total.

Weighted exact expectation
[2,2] has probability 0.45²=.2025, mean 2 and contribution 0.405. [40,40] has probability 0.02²=.0004, mean 40 and contribution 0.016. All 25 probabilities sum 1; the weighted sum of means is 5.3. Averaging unique mean values equally would describe a different model. Exact enumeration is capped; beyond the cap the UI does not claim an exact result. S² at n=1 is undefined.
14–16 minutes · Transfer

Separate n from R

Return to n=3 and record the theoretical SE before changing n. Then compare fresh n=12. If comparing empirical plots, use the same stated R for both fresh cohorts. The core two-minute task is the theoretical-SE comparison; extra simulation is optional. Ask for a prediction before changing n rather than judging width from a few random points.

“What happens to μ, E(X̄) and SE when we quadruple n? What if we only quadruple R?”

The theoretical comparison
μ and E(X̄) stay 5.3. SE=σ/√n halves:3.590265→1.795132. Changing R alone leaves theoretical SE unchanged. The original skewed population stays skewed. Its sample mean is not exactly Normal for every small n, and n=30 is not a universal guarantee.
16–18 minutes · Exit check

Ask for an explanation, not a button sequence

Use questions 1–3 from the student transfer sheet below. Ask learners to identify what exists before drawing, what changes next time, and what one point means. A correct choice with no reason is incomplete evidence. If time allows, use question 4 to compare a wasteful but unbiased rule with the mean.

Original transfer · student sheet

Can you use the idea in a new model?

New fictional model: each independent draw returns 1 point with probability 0.75 or 5 points with probability 0.25. Sampling is with replacement. We use n=4 draws to estimate the fixed population mean μ. A realised sample is [1,1,5,1]. These questions are original teaching material, not copied exam questions. Use extra paper for optional calculations.

  1. Find μ. Identify a parameter, an estimator before drawing and its observed estimate for this realised sample. What does the next dot in the mean-distribution strip represent?
  2. We repeat this four-draw experiment 1000 rather than 100 times. Which changes: the theoretical SE of one future sample mean, the Monte Carlo depiction, both or neither? Give a reason.
  3. Keep the rule X̄, but change n=4 to n=16. Describe the population mean, E(X̄) and SE. Does the next estimate have to equal μ?
  4. Compare rules T₁=X̄, T₂=X₁ and T₃=X̄+1 as estimators of μ. Which are unbiased? Does using all four observations automatically remove the offset in T₃? Optional: calculate each theoretical SE.
  5. For n=2, list the four ordered samples. Give their probabilities and show that the probability-weighted expected sample mean equals μ. Explain why four equally weighted cases would be wrong.
  6. Follow-on CI question: after an interval is computed, does “95% confidence” assign 95% probability to fixed μ lying in that realised interval? Must exactly 95 out of 100 repeated intervals cover? Explain both claims.
Teacher answers · original transfer sheet
  1. μ=.75×1+.25×5=2. Parameter μ; estimator X̄; observed estimate (1+1+5+1)/4=2. One dot is the mean computed from one complete sample of four observations, not an individual observation. Equality here is coincidence, not a requirement.
  2. Only the Monte Carlo depiction becomes more precise. n/model/rule determine the distribution of one estimator; R counts how often we simulate it.
  3. μ and E(X̄) stay 2. SE halves when n quadruples. The next observed estimate need not equal 2.
  4. E(T₁)=E(T₂)=2, so both unbiased. E(T₃)=3, bias+1; larger n does not remove it. Population variance=.75(1−2)²+.25(5−2)²=3. At n=4: SE(T₁)=SE(T₃)=√3/2≈.866025; SE(T₂)=√3≈1.732051. Adding a constant changes expectation, not variance.
  5. [1,1],[1,5],[5,1],[5,5] have probabilities 9/16,3/16,3/16,1/16 and means 1,3,3,5. Weighted expectation=(9+9+9+5)/16=2. Equal weighting would give 3 and ignore the declared probabilities.
  6. No to both claims. In this frequentist model μ is fixed; repeated samples give random endpoints. The method's nominal coverage concerns repetitions under its conditions; the realised interval either covers or misses. Finite coverage fluctuates, and approximation error can matter. Unbiasedness alone does not guarantee 95% interval coverage.
